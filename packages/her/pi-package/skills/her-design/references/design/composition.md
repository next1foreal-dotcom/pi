# Composition under the limit

Source: Geoff Teehan, *Limit the number of details* (Lightspark, 2026) — the constitution's second half: how to compose once you refuse to add.

## What counts as a detail

Every feature, control, mode, state, exception, and behavior — anything someone must design and someone must understand. Not just visual polish. The budget is attention, and **attention does not scale with the number of knobs**: three controls can each get six months of care; thirty become management. Cost is quadratic — two buttons are two buttons *plus the relationship between them*.

## Entry test — complexity must earn its way in

Before a detail exists, it answers two questions (ask them at the wireframe gate, not after):

1. **Has it earned its way in?** A 747 cockpit earns its complexity; a thermostat does not. Every added thing is a small mortgage — interest paid for years.
2. **Which five things are used most?** Those five get a permanent, unmoving place — never buried behind glass or menus. Choosing the five is the hard half.

Additions always have an advocate; removals have an enemy. So cutting is procedural — a gate question — not a mood.

## Four moves that compose under the limit

1. **Concentrate complexity.** When the problem is genuinely complex, do not spread it across the surface. Route interaction through one primary control and make that control extraordinary (a thousand songs, one wheel). One thing polished past reason beats ten things polished politely.
2. **Few controls become a language.** Controls may change jobs, but meaning stays constant (OP-1: four encoders, color-coded — turn the blue knob, the blue thing on screen changes). Learning is allowed — instruments take time — but learning comes from consistency, **never from labels or explanatory copy**.
3. **Borrow a known mental model.** The best control needs no explanation because there is nothing to misunderstand (MUJI CD player = a pull-chain light). A small idea beats a white rectangle; nothing-to-explain beats minimal-looking.
4. **What you cut buys care.** Each removal returns time — for typography, three words, two pixels, the sound, the annoyance that appears on the twentieth use. **The goal is not the fewest things; it is few enough things that every one gets deep care.**
